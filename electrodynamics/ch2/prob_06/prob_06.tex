\title{Problem 2.6}
\author{Gabriel Decker}


\begin{document}


\maketitle
\begin{flushleft}


In this problem, we find the electric field a distance $z$ above the center of a circular disk of charge with charge density $\sigma$ and radius $R$.
For this problem, define the coordinate system such that the center of the disk is at the origin, the ring exists in the $xy$-plane, and the $\hat{z}$ direction points from the origin to the point of interest.
Since we are dealing with a surface of charge, will use the following equation to find our electric field.
$$\vec{E}(\vec{r})=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\int\frac{\sigma(\vec{r'})}{\scripty{r}^2}\hat{\scripty{r}}\,da'$$\\~\\

We will use the following result from problem 2.5, where we found the electric field a distance $z$ above the center of a ring of line charge with radius $r$.
$$\vec{E}(z,r)=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{r\lambda}{(r^2+z^2)^{3/2}}\int_0^{2\pi}-r\hat{s}+z\hat{z}\,d\phi$$
$$\implies\vec{E}(z,r)=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{2\pi r\lambda z}{(r^2+z^2)^{3/2}}\hat{z}$$\\~\\

You can safely make the assumption that we will treat our disk as a bunch of rings and sum up the total electric field for all the infinitesimal rings with radii between zero and $R$, but I will demonstrate that this assumption works.\\~\\

Let's start by finding how $\lambda$ is related to $\sigma$.
$\sigma$ is total charge $Q$ divided by the total surface area $\pi R^2$, so $\sigma=Q/\pi R^2$.
It also follows that the density is the same for infinitesimal portions of the disk, so $\sigma=dq/dA$, or $dq=\sigma dA$.
If we first think of the disk as a summation of infinitesimal rings (as in problem 2.5), we can consider things rings with radius $r$ that will take a sliver of the area of the disk.
The charge of that ring is the charge density $\lambda$ times the circumference $2\pi r$.
So, an infinitesimal ring of charge with radius $r$ has charge, $dq=2\pi r\lambda$.
The rings can also be described as slivers of a disk with radius $r$ being subtracted from a disk of radius $(r+dr)$.
This has area:
$$dA=\pi(r+dr)^2-\pi r^2$$
$$\implies dA=\pi(r^2+2rdr+dr^2)-\pi r^2$$
$$\implies dA=2\pi rdr$$
(neglecting the doubly infinitesimal $dr^2$).
So, representing the charge that way, since $dq=\sigma dA$, this implies that $dq=2\pi\sigma rdr$.
We have two ways to represent the same $dq$, so we can equate them.
$$2\pi r\lambda=2\pi r\sigma dr$$
Finally, since our problem gives us a charge density $\sigma$, we can plug that into our $\vec{E}(z,r)$ equation to use to solve our integral later.
$$\implies\vec{E}(z,r)=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{2\pi r\sigma zdr}{(r^2+z^2)^{3/2}}\hat{z}$$\\~\\

Now, back to our original integral.
It turns out that our $\scripty{r}$ and $\hat{\scripty{r}}$ are the same as in problem 2.5, except now our $r$ varies, unlike the constant $R$ ring.
So, $\scripty{r}=\sqrt{r^2+z^2}$ and $\hat{\scripty{r}}=(-r\hat{s}+z\hat{z})/\scripty{r}$.
$\hat{s}$ is the same as in problem 2.5 (and cylindrical coordinates).
We will paramaterize $da'$ in polar coordinates, so $da'=rdrd\phi$ with $r$ going from zero to $R$ and $\phi$ from zero to $2\pi$.\\~\\

This all turns our integral into the following.
$$\vec{E}(\vec{r})=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\int\frac{\sigma(\vec{r'})}{\scripty{r}^2}\hat{\scripty{r}}\,da'$$
$$\implies\vec{E}(\vec{r})=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\sigma\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^R \frac{-r\hat{s}+z\hat{z}}{(r^2+z^2)^{3/2}}\,rdrd\phi$$
$$\implies\vec{E}(\vec{r})=\int_0^R\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{r\sigma}{(r^2+z^2)^{3/2}}\int_0^{2\pi} (-r\hat{s}+z\hat{z})\,d\phi dr$$\\~\\

By the result from 2.5 shown at the top of my solution (and the solution in terms of $\sigma$ and the fact that $\lambda=\sigma dr$), this implies the following.
$$\implies\vec{E}(\vec{r})=\int_0^R\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{2\pi r\sigma z}{(r^2+z^2)^{3/2}}\,dr\,\hat{z}$$
$$\implies\vec{E}(\vec{r})=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}(2\pi\sigma z)\hat{z}\int_0^R\frac{r}{(r^2+z^2)^{3/2}}\,dr$$
Using the substitution: $u=r^2+z^2$, so $du=2r\,dr$,
$$\implies\vec{E}(\vec{r})=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}(\pi\sigma z)\hat{z}\int_{z^2}^{R^2+z^2}\frac{1}{(u)^{3/2}}\,du$$
$$\implies\vec{E}(\vec{r})=-\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}(\pi\sigma z)\hat{z}\cdot2(\frac{1}{\sqrt{u}})\Big|_{z^2}^{R^2+z^2}$$
$$\implies\vec{E}(\vec{r})=-\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}(\pi\sigma z)\hat{z}\cdot2(\frac{1}{\sqrt{R^2+z^2}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{z^2}})$$
$$\implies\vec{E}(\vec{r})=\frac{\sigma}{2\epsilon_0}z\left(\frac{1}{z}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{R^2+z^2}}\right)\hat{z}$$
$$\implies\vec{E}(z)=\frac{\sigma}{2\epsilon_0}\left(1-\frac{z}{\sqrt{R^2+z^2}}\right)\hat{z}$$\\~\\

As $R\rightarrow\infty$, this implies that $\vec{E}$ is constant at all points above the disk (or infinite sheet of charge).


\end{flushleft}
