\title{Problem 2.5}
\author{Gabriel Decker}


\begin{document}


\maketitle
\begin{flushleft}


In this problem, we find the electric field a distance $z$ above the center of a ring of charge with charge density $\lambda$ and radius $R$.
For this problem, define the coordinate system such that the center of the ring is at the origin, the ring exists in the $xy$-plane, and the $\hat{z}$ direction points from the origin to the point of interest.
Since we are dealing with a line of charge, will use the following equation to find our electric field.
$$\vec{E}(\vec{r})=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\int\frac{\lambda(\vec{r'})}{\scripty{r}^2}\hat{\scripty{r}}\,d\ell'$$\\~\\

In order to evaluate this integral, we have several things to decide.
First, we know that $\hat{\scripty{r}}=\vec{\scripty{r}}/\scripty{r}$ and our line charge density $\lambda$ is constant, so this turns the integral into the following.
$$\vec{E}(\vec{r})=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\lambda\int\frac{\vec{\scripty{r}}}{\scripty{r}^3}\,d\ell'$$\\~\\

You can use a symmetry argument to simplify this problem and only have to find the $\hat{z}$ dependence of $\vec{E}$, but I will show why this argument is valid by not using it and coming to the same conclusion.
Since we're dealing with a system centered around a central axis that extends to a height $z$, and our $xy$-plane's system is circular, cylindrical coordinates are a good fit for this system.
So, our $\hat{z}$ coordinate will remain the same, but our circle will be represented essentially by polar coordinates.
In cylindrical coordinates, position is described by $\vec{r}(s,z)=s\hat{s}+z\hat{z}$, where $s$ represents the shortest distance to the central axis.\\~\\

For each infinitesimal segment in the circular charge, $\vec{\scripty{r}}$ is the following.
$$\vec{\scripty{r}}=\vec{r}-\vec{r'}$$
$$\implies\vec{\scripty{r}}=0\hat{s}+z\hat{z}-\left(R\hat{s}+0\hat{z}\right)$$
$$\implies\vec{\scripty{r}}=-R\hat{s}+z\hat{z}$$
where $\hat{s}$ points from the central axis to the infintesimal charge (along the shortest path, which is in the $xy$-plane).
Note that $\hat{s}$ is different for each bit of charge, but everything else is the same.
We can also get the magnitude, which is the same for every point.
$$\implies\scripty{r}=\sqrt{R^2+z^2}$$\\~\\

Plugging this into our integral, we get the following.
$$\vec{E}(\vec{r})=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\lambda\int\frac{-R\hat{s}+z\hat{z}}{(R^2+z^2)^{3/2}}\,d\ell'$$\\~\\

We can parameterize the ring of charge in the following way.
An infinitesimal segment of charge $d\ell'$ is a small segment of the circle's arc.
This is given by $d\ell'=Rd\phi$ since we have a circle.
Since our ring extends all the way around the circle of radius $R$, our $\phi$ runs from zero to $2\pi$.
Therefore, our integral becomes the following.
$$\implies\vec{E}(\vec{r})=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\lambda\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{-R\hat{s}+z\hat{z}}{(R^2+z^2)^{3/2}}R\,d\phi$$
$$\implies\vec{E}(\vec{r})=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{R\lambda}{(R^2+z^2)^{3/2}}\int_0^{2\pi}-R\hat{s}+z\hat{z}\,d\phi$$
We can split this integral into two.
$$\implies\vec{E}(\vec{r})=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{R\lambda}{(R^2+z^2)^{3/2}}\left[-R\int_0^{2\pi}\hat{s}\,d\phi+z\hat{z}\int_0^{2\pi}\,d\phi\right]$$
$$\implies\vec{E}(\vec{r})=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{R\lambda}{(R^2+z^2)^{3/2}}\left[-R\int_0^{2\pi}\hat{s}\,d\phi+2\pi z\hat{z}\right]$$\\~\\

Solving the last integral requires understanding $\hat{s}$.
This vector always points from the center to the charge, so it is reasonable to assume that if you are looking at all angles, the total vector would end up being zero.
This can be proven by converting $\hat{s}$ back to cartesian coordinates.
$\hat{s}=\cos(\phi)\hat{x}+\sin(\phi)\hat{y}$.
$$\implies\vec{E}(\vec{r})=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{R\lambda}{(R^2+z^2)^{3/2}}\left[-R\int_0^{2\pi}\cos(\phi)\hat{x}+\sin(\phi)\hat{y}\,d\phi+2\pi z\hat{z}\right]$$
$$\implies\vec{E}(\vec{r})=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{R\lambda}{(R^2+z^2)^{3/2}}\left[-R\left(\sin(\phi)\hat{x}-\cos(\phi)\hat{y}\right)\Big|_0^{2\pi}+2\pi z\hat{z}\right]$$
$$\implies\vec{E}(\vec{r})=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{R\lambda}{(R^2+z^2)^{3/2}}\left[-R\left(\sin(2\pi)\hat{x}-\cos(2\pi)\hat{y}\right)-\left(\sin(0)\hat{x}-\cos(0)\hat{y}\right)+2\pi z\hat{z}\right]$$
$$\implies\vec{E}(\vec{r})=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{R\lambda}{(R^2+z^2)^{3/2}}\left[-R\left(0\hat{x}-1\hat{y}\right)-\left(0\hat{x}-1\hat{y}\right)+2\pi z\hat{z}\right]$$
$$\implies\vec{E}(\vec{r})=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{R\lambda}{(R^2+z^2)^{3/2}}\left[2\pi z\hat{z}\right]$$
$$\implies\vec{E}(\vec{r})=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{2\pi R\lambda z}{(R^2+z^2)^{3/2}}\hat{z}$$


\end{flushleft}
